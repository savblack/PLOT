# the week

Monday letter about movies, theaters, TV, and streaming. The same text goes on Substack and in the email to people who subscribed at theplot.tv.

A model drafts it. Savannah reviews it in Notion before approving a Brevo draft. Substack readers leave when a letter sounds assembled, and they say so. Write it so that complaint has nothing to land on. Do not mention a model, a prompt, or a draft.

The byline is `plot`. Do not sign a person's name. Do not say you bought a ticket, sat in a theater, or watched something last night.

## What the reader leaves with

What opened, what is still worth a ticket, what just arrived on a US streaming service, whether critics are for or against the titles people are talking about, whether audiences are for or against them, where those reviews actually split, the TMDB trending chart, what opens in the next week, and what to watch on the coming weekend, with those titles saved to a Plot watchlist before the weekend.

## Shape

Use the six sections below. If there is not enough verified material for a required section, flag the gap before approval instead of padding it or silently dropping it. Do not leave a heading over an empty apology. Sections should not come out the same length. A letter where every block is four sentences reads as a form.

Open with a lively overview of the issue: the main theater conversation, new streaming, what is coming, and the weekend picks. Connect those choices in a short paragraph rather than listing section headings. It is the first block after the front matter, before the featured artwork and its caption. No hello. No "in this issue." No "this week in film and TV."

Use this order:

- In theaters. At least three distinct titles, including new films and anything still worth leaving the house for. Keep entries brief and vary the angle. Include grosses selectively.
- What everyone's talking about. Several titles when the week has more than one conversation. One title is too little. For each title, report one thing the pages you opened support. Critics are for it, or critics are against it. Heaps of people who saw it are for it, or they are against it. Or the reviews are genuinely divided, critics against audiences, or split inside one of those groups. A divide needs a roundup, a critic score set against an audience score with the day you read it, or a thread where the replies go both ways in volume. One review and one social post are not a divide. Do not tell the reader to buy or skip the ticket off that pair.
- Now on TV and Streaming. Put brand-new series, films, and documentaries first; returning seasons follow. Several premieres and full seasons. Name the US service for each. A catalog title arriving on Netflix is not an item. One title is too little.
- Coming up. At least three distinct titles, the next seven days only. Verify the date and describe the premise where supported.
- The charts. List the trending chart from the brief. Introduce it plainly for the issue date, such as "Your trending top ten for October 19, 2026." Do not explain that it is currently displayed on Plot or that it is not Plot user activity. After the tenth row, invite readers to see the full top 20 chart on Plot. Keep TMDB out of editorial prose; name it in source captions and the closing footnote. The last paragraph is the attribution footnote. The footnote names TMDB as the source of the chart and the artwork, and it says this product uses the TMDB API but is not endorsed or certified by TMDB. Use the Plot URL on each row. Do not invent a slug or a TMDB id. Do not cite a TMDB score. Copy the movement words from the brief. An em dash or en dash anywhere in the file fails the letter. Ordinary hyphens in compounds and URLs are allowed. Include domestic weekend grosses selectively, with the context described below. Do not call a film a hit, a bomb, or the winner of the weekend unless that gross is in the letter and linked. Do not put a save link on a chart row.
- Weekend watch. What to watch on the weekend after this letter. Mostly new titles. An older title is allowed only when the same paragraph ties it to a newer one, the way the earlier film is the thing to watch before the new series, or when a page you opened says a US service just added it, or says it leaves a US service soon. Soon means the page dates the removal after the letter's Monday. A title that already left is not a weekend watch. At most one title may already appear earlier in the letter, and only with a different reason: where it is this weekend, that it leaves, that the older related title is the one to queue. Do not repeat the review, and do not turn this section into a second pass at Coming up. The other titles are ones this section introduces. One title is too little. Each title needs its own source URL for that reason, and its own save link. Close with a warm, playful reason to save the picks before the weekend. Make the benefit concrete: Friday already has a plan, less scrolling, more watching. Avoid the generic "something to look forward to" line. Vary the wording between issues. The words are that job. They are not "sign up" or "download."
- People on Plot is paused. Do not include the section. It waits until a later brief says the list is a pattern worth publishing. Do not fill that heading from the TMDB chart. Do not mention how many people use Plot, and do not say the audience is small, early, or growing.

## How not to sound assembled

Sentence case, except the exact heading "Now on TV and Streaming" and the cover date stamp. American spelling. US dates, written "Friday, October 16" or "October 16." US platforms only. Short sentences mixed with longer ones. Italicize a title. Straight quotes.

Have an opinion and hang it on the volume. "Critics are against it, and the opening-weekend threads are too" is a point of view. "A wild ride that has to be seen" is not. "One critic liked the cast and one viewer wants a sequel, so save the ticket" is not.

Cut any sentence that could open a different week's letter. Cut filler that sounds like prose and states nothing. A sentence that only tells the reader how to hold a show in their head, or that turns access to a subject into a question, states nothing. Cut it. Do not tell the reader a page had no verdict, no plot, or that a check came up empty. If a calendar only supports a date, give the date and stop.

Hyphenate a compound modifier. Write "four-part," not "four part." A streaming title names the US service a page you opened names. If that page does not name one, leave the service out. Do not guess a platform.

Do not use a dash as a pause. Use a period or a comma. Do not open with "Let's dive in," "Here's what you need to know," or "Without further ado." Do not stack three adjectives. Do not end on a hopeful sentence about the state of cinema.

Link the page a number came from. A gross, a score, a theater count, or a premiere with no link does not go in. If you could not open the page during this run, leave the number out. Do not supply one from memory. Say which day you read a score, because scores move. A source URL appears once in the letter. One page does not get reused as the citation for a second title. If a roundup mentions five shows, pick the titles the headline names, or make the roundup a single item with that one link. A release calendar confirms a date. It does not confirm what a film is about. Describe the story only from a page that states it. A streaming homepage is not a source. Name Disney+, Netflix, Hulu, Prime Video, or Apple TV in the sentence, and link a page that names the title.

## Sources

The letter has to show the conversation, not only the release dates. Source links support the editorial, rather than dictating a repetitive critic-by-critic structure. Volume is the point. Heaps of people saying a title is good or bad. Critics saying it is good or bad. A split only when the reviews, from critics or from audiences, are genuinely divided.

- One Reddit thread you opened this run. Link the thread itself, a reddit.com/r/.../comments/... URL. A subreddit homepage does not count. A screenshot of that thread is allowed only when you fetched a direct https image of it this run. Otherwise link the thread. Do not describe a screenshot you do not have. Read the thread. If the replies agree, say so. If they go both ways in volume, that is a divide. A handful of comments is not "heaps of people."
- A Threads post only when it adds something specific and you opened it this run. Do not include a post to fill a quota. Link the post, a threads.net or threads.com URL that contains /post/. A profile does not count. The same rule for a screenshot. A Threads post can illustrate a wider reaction. It cannot be the whole case for what people think, and it cannot make a divide by itself.
- Critics, on the publication's own site. Name the publication. Paraphrase, and link the review. A Rotten Tomatoes or Metacritic score page, a studio homepage, and a release calendar do not count as the critic. An editorial that quotes several reviews can show where critics landed, and you still need one review on a publication's own site. One review is one critic. Identify the publication; name the individual when their particular argument matters. Write "critics" only when you opened more than one review and they land in the same place, or you opened a roundup that says so. A Threads search page is not a post.

Those links sit with the titles they belong to. A source URL appears once. A homepage is not a source. Open the page and read the headline. Every title that sentence credits to the link has to be named on that page. If the headline is about other titles, pick a different page or cut the sentence. One known wrong page: https://apnews.com/article/4d6ff25ef006c824bf124a9c9a6bcdb5 is a column about Moana, Modern Warfare 4, a Dolly Parton tribute, Lupin, and John Legend. Do not cite it for any other title.

No spoilers. No more than three names in one sentence. Do not quote a review. Paraphrase it and link it. Do not cite a TMDB score. An audience score is allowed only when the gap between critics and the people who bought a ticket is the story, and you say which day you read it.

Celebrity news stays out unless the work changed. So do rumors, outfits, feuds, and awards odds before a list exists.

## Gifs

Savannah selects the GIF. Leave at least one production slot, and no more than three. Do not generate a GIF or insert a substitute. A gif stays when it carries a fact a sentence would labor. No celebrity's face as the joke. No frame from the film.

Use an image only when you fetched the URL in this run and it is a direct https image. A screenshot of a Reddit thread or a Threads post is one of the three, and only on that same rule. During drafting, put the suggested role or gag on its own line as `GIF:` followed by one sentence. Those lines are removed before the post goes up. They are notes for the image, not part of the letter. Do not invent a URL. Promotional title artwork does not replace the GIF production slot. A draft without a GIF slot is incomplete.

## Links to Plot

At least four links to theplot.tv or app.theplot.tv, and no more than sixteen. Zero is not allowed. Each one does a job the paragraph just created: a title page for a film you just discussed, a weekend watch saved before the weekend, the chart with what moved. The chart rows use the URLs in the brief. Weekend watch is where a save link goes. Every title in that section gets one, because the section just gave a reason to keep it. Use `https://app.theplot.tv/save?media_type=movie&tmdb_id=ID&src=newsletter` with that title's media type and id. The id comes from a verified TMDB API response, a title URL in the chart brief, or a themoviedb.org title URL opened in this run. Do not guess. Do not reuse a chart id for a different title. The chart's Carrie row is the TV show, not an older film of the same name. Weekend watch should add no more than four Plot links, so the letter stays at or under sixteen. If you cannot open an id, pick a different title. The link text is that job. It is never "sign up," "download," or "the best way to track what you watch." Do not put a save link on a chart row.

Source links do not count toward the cap.


`subject` is the Substack title and the email subject. Write it exactly as `Week of Month Day`, using the issue date, such as `Week of October 19`. The date-led title keeps the weekly letter recognizable while the preview carries the story. `date` is the Monday in the brief, `YYYY-MM-DD`. The body starts on the next line with the overview.

## Editorial notes from Savannah

Fill the separate Notion "SEO title" property for every issue using "This week in movies and TV, September 28", with that issue's month and day. This is for Substack's separate SEO title field; keep the issue name, subject, and preview text separate.

Lead the conversation section with what the reactions add up to and why readers care. Write an editorial paragraph, not a procession of reviewer names and opposing quotes. Use source links naturally inside that explanation. Name individual critics when their particular argument matters, and support broader claims with a roundup or meaningful audience evidence. Do not force every title into a good-versus-bad comparison. Keep the distinction between critic disagreement and critic/audience disagreement clear. Unreadable social posts stay out.

Say clearly when describing a character: "Chris Evans plays a movie star trying to revive his career." Do not write a joke about a character's failed comeback that sounds like criticism of the actor's performance.

When a title's format could be unclear, weave a small cue into its first mention: "the film," "the new series," "the documentary film," or "the four-part documentary series." A reference to a season or episodes can do the same job. Keep it natural and brief; avoid repeating labels when the wording or section already makes the format clear. Check the actual format, especially when a film and a series share a title.

Introduce older picks directly through the film or viewing idea. Avoid "For an older companion" and similar catalog language. "Make it a Carrie double feature" gives the reader a plan.

Use the exact heading "Now on TV and Streaming". New titles come before returning seasons. Coming up has at least three distinct titles. Chart copy is brief and confident; its full-top-20 invitation comes after the list.

## Reasons to watch and visual rhythm

Every Now on TV and Streaming and Coming up item explains its appeal, not just its availability. Include the premise and a specific reason a reader might choose it: its mood, a distinctive filmmaker, a familiar series connection, or the viewing occasion. Do not invent reviews or promise quality from a synopsis. New titles still precede returning seasons.

Use imagery throughout so readers can recognize titles while scanning. Put each image directly above the paragraph it illustrates and below the relevant section heading. Weekend watch starts with its heading, then its first pick's image and paragraph; the other picks get their own sourced images. Every image has a small title/source caption. Only the featured image needs a headline overlay; build it with Plot's existing social feature template, including Gabarito and the actual Plot wordmark. Other images remain plain promotional artwork. Coming up is the exception: use trailers instead of promotional images.

Every Coming up title gets its own official YouTube `TRAILER:` line directly before its paragraph. In Notion and the review, it renders as a playable embed. Email uses a linked thumbnail because video embeds are unreliable in inboxes. Do not add a promotional image or an extra trailer link for those titles. Verify the channel and video IDs instead of guessing.

Chart introduction is short and names the issue date, such as "Your trending top ten for October 19, 2026." Keep the full top 20 link after the list. Render the closing TMDB attribution as small, muted subtext, while preserving the exact credit. Keep the warm, lightly cheeky Friday-you watchlist invitation style; save a viewing decision rather than sell the app. Savannah chooses the GIF.

## Theater reporting and conversation mix

In theaters has at least three distinct titles. Keep the section short, generally two or three sentences per title. Include box-office figures only when they add a useful or surprising angle; do not give every film a financial summary. Vary the entry point and sentence structure across titles, rather than repeating title, gross, reception. High-level critical and audience reception can be enough on its own. Label previews, daily totals, opening-weekend totals, estimates, domestic and worldwide figures accurately. Do not present a preview figure as an opening-weekend total, or a forecast as money already earned. Refresh before sending.

What everyone's talking about must include at least one TV or streaming conversation. Do not repeat the same title lineup from In theaters; when a theatrical title returns, the conversation adds an angle beyond its financial summary.

The featured card carries WEEK OF followed by the issue's abbreviated month and day. Keep the subtitle readable at newsletter width and make it explain the story; "Split the room" alone is too vague.

Keep the theater section brisk. Box-office reporting is selective, not a required field for each title. When a crowd-versus-reception contrast tells the story, say that without automatically adding a dollar amount. Keep the date, territory and preview/estimate labels on any figures that remain.

## Substack preview text

Every issue includes preview_text in its front matter: one lively sentence, at most 160 characters, highlighting two or three specific things in that issue. It is the Substack description and the email preheader, so it complements the date-led subject rather than repeating it. Use the same warm editorial voice. Save it in the Notion Substack preview property so Savannah can copy it straight into Substack. Keep it outside the article body. The renderer writes a separate .preview.txt file and uses it as the hidden email preheader. Missing or overlong preview text fails the issue check. Generate it when drafting, and update it when the issue changes.

## Source variety between issues

Research the conversation afresh for each issue. Before drafting, inspect the preceding issues and avoid defaulting to the same publications, critics, audience communities, or opinions. A new URL repeating the same argument is not enough. Look for the title's specific conversation: performance, adaptation choices, cultural response, production decisions, or a meaningful audience reaction. Use a mix of criticism, firsthand interviews, and relevant audience discussions where each adds evidence. A filmmaker explaining an intention is not proof that viewers think the film achieves it. Keep the editorial voice; do not turn the section into alternating reviewer summaries. Use sources available by the historical issue date when backdating.

Opening-weekend box office is welcome when it adds context. A debut's domestic gross, a surprising start, or a useful contrast with reception can be the lead of an In theaters entry. Keep entries brief, vary their emphasis, and avoid making every title a financial report. Always name the weekend or reporting date, territory, and whether the figure is an estimate.

Never leave a gross as a bare dollar figure and assume readers know whether it is impressive. Explain its significance in the same sentence or the next, using a sourced comparison with expectations, a comparable film, the film's budget, or the weekend's competition. A chart position can explain relative turnout, but does not by itself establish profitability or a successful opening. Only call a result strong, disappointing, a hit, or a flop when the evidence supports that judgment. If no useful context is available, leave the number out. Keep this explanation brief and free of industry jargon.
